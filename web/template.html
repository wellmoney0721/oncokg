Entity resolution · knowledge graph · grounded retrieval

Reading messy trial text into a drug graph

Clinical trial registries describe the same molecule as Tarceva, OSI-774, erlotinib hydrochloride and Gemicitabine—with a typo. This pipeline turns that text into a knowledge graph of companies, drugs, targets and trials in non-small cell lung cancer, and reports what it got wrong.

01 · The measurement

What the numbers are worth

Precision was measured on three separate hand-scored samples. The development sample shaped the rules, so its number is optimistic by construction; the held-out test sample, scored as it was labelled, is the honest one. Three audit rounds have since used the test rows to find defects. The per-tier rates below are the repaired pipeline on the rows whose resolution did not change — and the rows that changed held every error, so these rates are survivors, not a measurement.

02 · The decision

Every string gets a tier, and a reason

A resolution is never a bare yes. Each one records how it was reached, and anything below the automatic threshold is routed to review instead of being accepted quietly. Blocked tiers are refusals the pipeline makes on purpose.

03 · The finding

The reference database is wrong too

Treating a curated vocabulary as ground truth is the easiest way to inherit its errors. ChEMBL lists avelumab, durvalumab and adebrelimab among atezolizumab's synonyms, and durvalumab placebo among durvalumab's. Each defect below was detected automatically and repaired before any record was matched against the dictionary.

06 · The answers

Retrieval that checks itself

Questions are resolved against the same dictionary the pipeline uses, so a question naming a trade name finds a graph that stores a ChEMBL id. The answer is then verified against its own evidence: names, drug-shaped words and numbers that do not appear in the retrieved set are reported rather than shipped. The check cannot verify a relation, a negation or a clinical claim made of ordinary words. With no language model key the evidence is rendered directly and the check does not apply; with one, the model sees the retrieved evidence and nothing else.

08 · Known defects

What is still broken

Unrepaired, and listed in full in the README. Leaving them listed is cheaper than a number that quietly assumes they do not exist.

  • The placebo rule needs a separator. Placebo Drug: Docetaxel Drug: Carboplatin has no + or and, so it reads as a reference arm and two genuinely administered drugs are dropped. The fix is to treat a repeated Drug: label as a separator.
  • The repaired pipeline has no held-out measurement. The test sample was used by three audit rounds; 26 of its 172 rows now resolve differently and are set aside as stale. Putting a number on the current pipeline needs a new sample, labelled by someone other than the author.
  • Coverage is capped by scope, not by skill. The vocabulary holds only drugs Open Targets links to NSCLC, so propranolol and Diklofenak Sodyum are correctly refused and still count against coverage. Widening to the full ChEMBL drug set is the obvious next step, and would need its own precision measurement.
  • One annotator, three independent re-scores. Every verdict is the author's; three reviews re-scored 167 deliberately difficult rows and disagreed on twelve, every one lenient. An agreement rate over the full sample is what would turn these numbers from defensible into solid.
  • Four tiers were never independently tested. FUZZY_AUTO, FUZZY_REVIEW, SALT_STRIPPED and CLEANED have so few distinct values that the development sample consumed all of them, so their rates are carried over from the data the rules were tuned on and should be read as optimistic.
  • The synonym clean-up errs toward refusal. Removing contaminated registry synonyms also removed some correct names (misspellings, code names a molecule record does not list); each removal is a finding in the quality report.
